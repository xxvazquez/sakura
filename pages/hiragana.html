んn — a syllabic nasal that doesn't belong to any row or column of the grid.

ん

ん shifts slightly depending on the sound after it — closer to "m" before b/m/p sounds — but it's always written the same way.

を

を is pronounced exactly like お ("o") in modern Japanese, even though it's written differently. It's used only as the object-marking particle and never starts a word.

Small っ (Sokuon)

A small っ has no sound of its own — it signals a brief pause that doubles the consonant right after it.

Long Vowels

Holding a vowel for two beats instead of one changes a word's meaning — usually spelled by doubling that vowel, except え and お, which are more often spelled with an い or う instead of repeating themselves.

Long vowel Usually written Example Romaji Meaning
あ (a) あ + あ お母さんおかあさん okāsan mother
い (i) い + い おじいさん  ojīsan grandfather
う (u) う + う 空港くうこう kūkō airport
え (e) え + い 先生せんせい sensei teacher
お (o) お + う 東京とうきょう Tōkyō Tokyo

え+い and お+う are the common spellings, not a rule without exceptions — a handful of everyday words spell the same long vowel as ええ or おお instead, including 大おおきい (ōkii, big) and おねえさん (onēsan, older sister). There's no shortcut for which words do this — they're learned individually as you meet them.
