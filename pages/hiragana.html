Long Vowels

Which kana lengthens a vowel depends on the row it follows — three patterns, one exception.

Pattern Japanese Romaji English
あ-row + あ お母さんおかあさん okāsan mother
い/え-row + い 先生せんせい sensei teacher
う/お-row + う 東京とうきょう Tōkyō Tokyo
exception: え-row + い stays "ei" 先生せんせい sensei, not sensē teacher

💡 Notes

Dakuten (゛)

Two small strokes added to certain consonants change them from unvoiced to voiced.

か→がka → ga さ→ざsa → za た→だta → da は→ばha → ba

Handakuten (゜)

A small circle added only to は-row characters changes them to a "p" sound.

は→ぱha → pa

Yōon (Combination Sounds)

A small ゃ, ゅ or ょ after an い-row character contracts the two into a single sound, rather than two separate syllables.

き + ゃ→きゃkya, not ki-ya し + ゅ→しゅshu, not shi-yu り + ょ→りょryo, not ri-yo

Small っ (Sokuon)

A small っ has no sound of its own — it signals a brief pause that doubles the consonant right after it.

学校がっこう
gak-kō, not ga-kō
school

Long Vowels

Hiragana marks a long vowel differently depending on the vowel it follows — see the table in Advanced Hiragana above for the three patterns and the えい exception.

を

を is pronounced exactly like お ("o") in modern Japanese, even though it's written differently. It's used only as the object-marking particle and never starts a word.

を=おboth "o"

ん

ん shifts slightly depending on the sound after it — closer to "m" before b/m/p sounds — but it's always written the same way.

散歩さんぽ
sanpo
walk
