Hiragana

📖 Hiragana is one of Japan's three writing systems, alongside katakana and kanji. It's used to write native Japanese words and grammatical elements — verb and adjective endings, particles like は and が, okurigana (the hiragana that follows a kanji's stem), and everyday words that don't have a common kanji form.

Each character represents one mora (a single beat of sound) rather than a full syllable in the English sense, and — unlike kanji — almost every hiragana has exactly one reading, making it the natural starting point for reading Japanese.

💡 Notes

Dakuten (゛)

  • Two small strokes added to certain consonants change them from unvoiced to voiced.
  • か → が (ka → ga), さ → ざ (sa → za), た → だ (ta → da), は → ば (ha → ba).

Handakuten (゜)

  • A small circle added only to は-row characters changes them to a "p" sound: は → ぱ (ha → pa).

Combination Sounds (Yōon)

  • A small ゃ, ゅ or ょ after an い-row character contracts the two into a single sound, rather than two separate syllables — きゃ (kya), しゅ (shu), りょ (ryo).

Small っ (sokuon)

  • A small っ has no sound of its own — it signals a brief pause that doubles the consonant right after it. 学校がっこう (gakkō, "school") is held on the k: gak-kō, not ga-kō.

Long Vowels

  • Hiragana marks a long vowel differently depending on the vowel: あ-row lengthens with あ (お母さんおかあさん, okāsan, "mother"), い/え-row usually with い (先生せんせい, sensei, "teacher"), and う/お-row usually with う (東京とうきょう, Tōkyō) — a long あ, う or お is romanized with a macron (ā, ū, ō), while えい stays "ei".

を

  • を is pronounced exactly like お ("o") in modern Japanese, even though it's written differently. It's used only as the object-marking particle and never starts a word.

ん

  • ん shifts slightly depending on the sound after it — closer to "m" before b/m/p sounds, as in 散歩さんぽ (sanpo, "walk") — but it's always written the same way.
